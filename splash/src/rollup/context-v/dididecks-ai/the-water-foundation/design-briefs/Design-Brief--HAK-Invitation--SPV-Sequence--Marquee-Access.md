# Design Brief — SPV Sequence, Marquee Access

> **Building this in Claude design?** The reasoning runs first because this
> sequence's argument is load-bearing and a builder who doesn't have it will
> produce a pretty logo wall that undercuts the deck. Then go to
> [Build spec](#build-spec--self-contained-handoff), which inlines every value,
> layout variant, and the imagery pipeline you're being asked to run.

## The job

Three things at once, and they pull against each other:

1. **Wow.** This is the deck's peak. It has to land as *these people have
   access we do not*.
2. **Make the SPVs known.** Five companies are raising now with direct access.
   The reader should finish the sequence knowing they can take a position this
   quarter.
3. **Bridge the timing gap.** The hybrid fund-of-funds will take months to a
   first close. Without something actionable in between, a warm prospect cools.

And underneath all three: prove **commercial judgment** and **impact at scale**
are the same capability, not a trade the reader has to make.

## The trap to avoid

The obvious build — an 18-logo grid with funding numbers — actively damages the
pitch. Three reasons:

1. **It reads as a VC deck that wandered into a water conversation.** The
   audience is family offices and family foundations who came for water
   resilience. A wall of Thiel-and-NVIDIA venture names, presented without
   framing, makes them ask *what am I actually being sold?*
2. **Enthusiasm is not judgment.** Anyone can list 18 exciting companies. A
   list demonstrates reading; a *structure* demonstrates a thesis. The reader's
   real question is "why these eighteen and not eighteen others," and only
   organisation answers it.
3. **It buries the ask.** Five of the eighteen are actionable. On a flat grid,
   that fact is a footnote. It's the whole point.

## The reframe that fixes all three

**The smartest capital in the world is already in the water. It just isn't
calling it water.**

Peter Thiel funding wave-powered AI data centres. In-Q-Tel and Lockheed Martin
on an electric seaglider cap table. CapitalG and NVIDIA in autonomous
earthmoving. GV in autonomous Navy ships. None of these were underwritten as
water investments — they were underwritten as AI infrastructure, defence
autonomy and industrial robotics. **The water exposure came free, and nobody
priced it.**

That single observation does everything the sequence needs:

- It makes the venture names *evidence for* the water thesis instead of a
  distraction from it.
- It positions TWF as seeing a category the generalists are entering blind —
  which is the only credible basis for claiming differentiated access.
- It makes returns and impact the same bet rather than a compromise, because
  the commercial thesis and the water thesis are the same underlying fact.

Everything below is in service of that sentence. If a slide doesn't advance it,
cut the slide.

## The sequence

Ten slides. Five carry the argument, four detail the portfolio, one is
strong-optional.

| # | Slot | Working title | Job | Keep if space is tight? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | 11 | The capital is already here | Reframe. Wow. | Yes — this is the reframe |
| **B** | 12 | Eighteen companies, one thesis | Judgment + impact | **Yes — the single most important slide** |
| **B1** | 13 | Ocean energy and compute | Detail, group one | Cut to appendix if needed |
| **B2** | 14 | Maritime autonomy and defence | Detail, group two | Cut to appendix if needed |
| **B′** | 15 | Moving dirt is a water technology | Proves the reframe | **Yes — this is the proprietary insight** |
| **B3** | 16 | Restoration and recovery | Detail, group four | Cut to appendix if needed |

| **C** | 18 | Five positions, open now | The ask | Yes |
| **D** | 19 | Two tracks | The mechanic / timing bridge | Yes |
| **E** | 20 | If they work | Impact at scale | Optional — fold into B if cut |

**On the four group data sheets (B1, B2, B3, B4).** The map at B shows eighteen
dots; only five get a card at C. Without these, thirteen companies are asserted
and never explained, and a reader who wants to know why a dot is on the page has
nowhere to go. They also do something the map can't: each opens with a **thesis
sentence** — the reason those companies belong together — which is what converts
a bucket into a claim.

They are the natural appendix if the deck runs long. Moving them behind C costs
nothing structurally; cutting them entirely leaves B unsupported.

Note the ordering: **B′ sits between B2 and B3**, at slot 15, rather than after
all four sheets. Earthworks is the fifth group, but it gets an argument instead
of a data sheet, and burying that argument at the end of four inventory slides
would waste it. Putting it mid-run also breaks up what would otherwise be four
near-identical layouts in a row.

**If only one slide survives: build B.** The 2×2 map with the five live
positions highlighted does all three jobs in a single frame — marquee access,
commercial judgment, and impact at scale — and it is the frame most likely to
be photographed.

**If two survive: B and B′.** B proves the Foundation can select. B′ proves it
can see something nobody else in the room can. Those are different claims and
the second one is rarer.

### Slide A — "The capital is already here"

**Argument:** you already trust these names. They're already in.

**Recommended layout — the investor name wall.** Twenty-four investor names set
large in the brand type, filling most of the frame as a justified type block.
No logos. Eight names set in `#5BCEFF`; the rest in `#DEEAE8`. One line beneath:

> Every name above sits on the cap table of a company in our portfolio map.
> None of them underwrote a water investment. All of them made one.

Why the type block and not logos: a wall of scraped logos looks like a
conference sponsor page and **implies endorsement of The Water Foundation**,
which none of these firms have given. Names set in the deck's own typeface are
a statement of fact about cap tables. Logos are a claim about relationships.
The distinction matters legally and it also looks better.

**Names, in this order** (rhythm matters more than ranking — long and short
alternate):

> Peter Thiel · Founders Fund · Google Ventures · CapitalG · NVIDIA ·
> In-Q-Tel · Lockheed Martin · Lowercarbon · Y Combinator · John Doerr ·
> Marc Benioff · L3Harris · Point72 · Seagate · 8VC · Eclipse · Northzone ·
> Max Levchin · Techstars · Builders Vision · Shield Capital · Dylan Field ·
> Mark Cuban · Katapult Ocean

Accent these eight: **Peter Thiel · Founders Fund · Google Ventures ·
CapitalG · NVIDIA · In-Q-Tel · Lockheed Martin · Lowercarbon**.

**Alternates worth generating:** (A2) three converging flows — *AI
infrastructure · maritime autonomy · climate resilience* — meeting on a water
mark, with investor names distributed along each flow; (A3) a large-number stat
bar (`~$1B` · `$350M+` · `$100M+` · `18` · `5`) with the names as a secondary
band. A2 is more beautiful and less immediate; A3 is more immediate and less
distinctive. Generate all three; A1 is the recommendation.

### Slide B — "Eighteen companies, one thesis" ← the important one

**Argument:** we didn't collect these, we selected them — and the selection
proves that commercial return and impact at scale point the same way.

**Recommended layout — the 2×2 map.**

- **X axis: Commercial return potential** → left "Proven economics", right
  "Exponential outcome"
- **Y axis: Impact at scale** → bottom "Meaningful", top "Systemic"
- **18 dots**, labelled with company names. The **5 LIVE** rendered as filled
  `#5BCEFF` markers at roughly 2× the diameter, each with a small ⭐; the other
  13 as hollow `#DEEAE8` rings.
- **The upper-right quadrant is labelled** *"Exponential and systemic — where
  we are taking positions."* Four of the five live names should land there.

Why this is the best slide in the deck: it answers "why these eighteen" with a
structure rather than an assertion; it makes *exponential returns* and *impact
at scale* orthogonal axes rather than a trade-off, which is the Foundation's
entire investment argument rendered as geometry; and it makes the five live
positions visually inevitable rather than announced.

⚠ **The axis positions are a real claim and must be client-owned.** Placing
RiverRecycle low on return or Qronon low on impact is a judgment TWF is
publishing. Build it with a defensible first pass, but treat every coordinate
as a draft for the client to move. Do not let a design tool assign positions on
vibes.

**Alternates worth generating:** (B2) **thesis columns** — three columns of six
under *Ocean Energy & Compute* / *Maritime Autonomy & Defence* / *Water Systems
& Restoration*, each an image card; safest, most readable, least memorable.
(B3) **image-card grid 6×3**, all eighteen with imagery; densest and most
"portfolio", weakest on judgment. Generate B2 and B3 as fallbacks — B3 is also
the natural appendix slide if the sequence needs one.

**Thesis grouping.** **Five groups**, and each one gets its own slide — four
data sheets plus B′ for earthworks:

| 13 · Ocean Energy & Compute | 14 · Maritime Autonomy & Defence | 15 · Earthworks & Groundwater | 16 · Restoration & Recovery | 17 · Water Intelligence & Markets |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Panthalassa ⭐ | REGENT Craft ⭐ | **Bedrock Robotics ⭐** | Seabound ⭐ | Waterplan |
| Bedrock Ocean | Blue Water Autonomy | **TerraFirma ⭐** | Coral Vita | Qronon |
| Terradepth | Seasats | | RiverRecycle | BlueLayer |
| | Albacore | | Clearbot | |
| | Blueye Robotics | | Axine Water | |

Three, five, two, five, three. The two-company group is the smallest and the
most important — see B′ below. Its smallness is an asset, not a balance problem:
the eye goes to the short column and asks why it exists, which is exactly the
question the next slide answers.

**Why restoration and intelligence are separate groups.** An earlier draft
lumped all eight into "Water Systems & Restoration," which was a bucket rather
than a thesis. The split is real and load-bearing: **Restoration & Recovery**
companies physically remove, destroy or rebuild something and charge someone for
it — a developer who needs a permit, a shipowner who needs compliance, a port
that needs clearing. **Water Intelligence & Markets** companies make water
legible to a balance sheet and sell into a disclosure regime that is already
mandatory in eight jurisdictions. Hardware that touches water, versus software
that prices it. Different buyers, different risk, different reason to believe.

Each group slide leads with its thesis sentence before any company appears. A
group that can't produce one shouldn't be a group.

### Slide B′ — "Moving dirt is a water technology" ← the proprietary insight

**Argument:** the cost of moving earth has always been the binding constraint on
landscape-scale water retention. Autonomous earthmoving removes it. Two of our
five live positions are, correctly understood, groundwater companies.

This is the best idea in the sequence and it does not appear in the source
research at all. Everything else in this deck is *selection* — a claim that TWF
picked well from a visible universe. This is *insight*: a claim that TWF
understands something about a company that the company's own investors have not
priced. CapitalG and NVIDIA underwrote Bedrock Robotics as construction
robotics. Nobody underwrote it as aquifer recharge.

**The mechanism, in four beats** — this is the slide's spine:

1. **Permaculture earthworks work.** Swales, keyline plowing, contour bunds,
   check dams and retention ponds slow runoff, hold it on the land, and let it
   percolate rather than flash off. The technique is old, well-understood, and
   not in dispute.
2. **They have never scaled, for one reason: dirt is expensive to move.**
   Landscape-scale contouring is priced per cubic metre of earth and per hour of
   operator time. At current cost, it pencils on a demonstration farm and
   nowhere else.
3. **Autonomous earthmoving collapses that cost.** Retrofit kits on machines
   already on site, running around the clock, with no operator constraint — the
   two companies attack it from opposite ends. Bedrock retrofits the contractor's
   existing fleet; TerraFirma *owns the contractor* and runs the jobs itself.
4. **Therefore: earthworks that were uneconomic become economic — at watershed
   scale.** Rainwater captured instead of lost. Runoff retained instead of
   flooding. Water tables recharged instead of drawn down.

**The line that lands it:**

> Every other investor on this cap table is buying construction robotics. We are
> buying the ability to reshape a watershed at a price that has never existed.

**Recommended layout — the four-beat mechanism, left to right**, as four panels
with connecting arrows, running the width of the frame. Each panel: a short
label, one line, and a simple diagram. The fourth panel is where the visual
payoff lives.

Diagrammatic suggestion for the panels:

```
1. THE TECHNIQUE     2. THE CONSTRAINT     3. THE UNLOCK        4. THE RESULT
   ▁▂▃ contour          $ per m³ of          autonomous          ░░░░ water table
   swales on a          earth moved —        retrofit, 24h       rising beneath a
   hillside             the whole reason     operation, no       contoured
   holding water        it never scaled      operator cap        landscape
```

Panel 4 should be a **cross-section**: a hillside in profile, contour swales on
the surface, and a visibly raised water table beneath, rendered as a band of
`#5FD7C5`. A before/after split within that panel — depleted table on the left,
recharged on the right — is the single most persuasive image available to this
sequence. If one illustration in this deck is worth commissioning properly
rather than scraping, it is this one.

Beneath the four panels, the two company cards side by side, labelled with how
they differ:

| Bedrock Robotics ⭐ | TerraFirma ⭐ |
|---|---|
| Retrofits the contractor's fleet | Owns the contractor |
| $350M+ raised · $270M Series B, CapitalG-led · NVIDIA NVentures | SpaceX alumni · live paying contracts today |
| Addressable fleet: every excavator already on a job site | Every job is revenue *and* training data |

**Alternates worth generating:** (B′2) a **single large cross-section** filling
the frame — hillside, swales, rising water table — with the four beats as short
captions around it and the two companies as a footer strip; more beautiful,
slower to read. (B′3) **before/after split-frame** — bare eroded slope left,
contoured water-holding landscape right, with the two companies named beneath;
simplest and most emotional, weakest on mechanism.

⚠ **Do not overclaim the timeline.** Neither company is doing permaculture
earthworks today; both are doing construction and site prep. The honest framing
is *capability unlocked*, not *work underway*. Phrase it as what becomes
possible and affordable, never as what is currently being built. A reader who
checks and finds these are construction-robotics companies should find the
slide's claim still standing.

### Slide C — "Five positions, open now"

**Argument:** here is the actionable set, and here is why each one is not a
maybe.

**Recommended layout — hero plus four.** Panthalassa takes the upper half as a
hero card (image left, content right); REGENT, Bedrock Robotics, TerraFirma and
Seabound run as four equal cards beneath. Asymmetry is the point — five equal
boxes reads as a catalogue, and Panthalassa is objectively the best story in
the set (Thiel, ~$1B, wave-powered AI compute at sea).

**Bedrock Robotics and TerraFirma must sit adjacent**, in positions two and
three of the lower row, joined by a hairline bracket above them labelled
`GROUNDWATER, VIA EARTHWORKS — see previous`. Having just spent a slide
establishing that these two are a paired thesis, separating them here undoes it.
The bracket also does useful work on its own: a reader who skipped B′ sees two
construction-robotics companies explicitly claimed as a water position and has
to go back and find out why.

**Per-card content — use exactly these fields, nothing more:**

| Field | Source | Length |
|---|---|---|
| Company name | — | — |
| Descriptor | "The opportunity" column | 4–7 words |
| Marquee signal | "Where they are" column, bolded fragment | ≤ 10 words |
| Status chip | `RAISING NOW` | fixed |
| Image | imagery pipeline below | 16:10 |

**Do not put the one-paragraph write-ups on any slide.** They are speaker notes
and appendix material. The three-line versions are the most that ever belongs
on the hero card, trimmed to two.

**The closing line for the slide** — this is the sentence that does the work,
lifted from the source material and worth keeping nearly verbatim:

> This is not a list of companies we admire. It is a list of positions we can
> take, now.

**Alternates worth generating:** (C2) five equal columns; (C3) five full-width
stacked rows, image left, content right — most readable, needs two slides.

### Slide D — "Two tracks"

**Argument:** the fund takes time; this doesn't. You can start before the first
close, and starting is how you learn whether you want the fund.

This slide does not exist in the source material and is the one the sequence
most needs. Without it the reader finishes impressed and with nothing to do.

**Recommended layout — two parallel horizontal tracks** running left to right
across the frame:

```
TRACK 1 — THE FUND        ░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░  first close ──▶  deployment
                          diligence · structuring · anchor commitments

TRACK 2 — SPVs            ██████▶   ██████▶   ██████▶
                          per-deal · months not years · you choose each one
```

Track 1 as a long hollow bar with the first-close milestone marked. Track 2 as
three or four short filled `#5BCEFF` bars beginning immediately — visibly
completing before Track 1's first milestone.

The visual argument is made by the bars alone: **you can be invested before the
fund exists.** Copy should be minimal; three short labels per track.

Beneath, three plain statements: per-deal participation · no blind pool · each
SPV is a standalone decision.

⚠ **Legal review required on this slide specifically.** Presenting available
positions to prospective investors is securities-adjacent, and the line between
"illustrative of pipeline" and "an offer" is exactly where this slide sits.
Build it; get counsel to set the framing language and any required disclaimer
before it goes out. Reserve footer space for a one-line disclaimer now rather
than re-laying-out later.

### Slide E — "If they work" (optional)

**Argument:** the impact case, stated as consequence rather than aspiration.

Four companies, four scale numbers, no hedging:

| Company | If it works |
|---|---|
| Bedrock Robotics + TerraFirma | Landscape-scale water retention at a cost that has never existed — **runoff held, water tables recharged** ⚠ needs a defensible number |
| Seabound | **100 million tonnes of CO₂** captured annually by 2040 |
| RiverRecycle | **4 billion kg** of riverine plastic reaches the ocean each year; target **3 million tonnes diverted by 2035** |
| Waterplan | **$425 billion** in water-related financial risk made visible; UN projects a **40% supply deficit by 2030** |
| Coral Vita | Reefs support **25% of marine life**, 1 billion livelihoods, **$2.7 trillion** in annual value |

**Layout:** five full-height columns, the number set enormous (~120px), the
company name small above it, one line of context below. Nothing else on the
slide. At five columns the number drops to ~100px.

⚠ **The earthworks column has no number yet, and it needs one** — it is the only
row here carrying a claim rather than a figure, and next to four hard numbers
that reads as the weak one. Candidates the client could source: hectares of
contoured landscape per machine-year at autonomous cost; cubic metres of runoff
retained per hectare of swale; or the current per-m³ earthmoving cost against a
projected autonomous cost, which makes the unlock arithmetic rather than
rhetorical. **The third is the strongest** — a before/after price per cubic
metre is the entire thesis in two numbers.

If the sequence must shrink, fold the earthworks and Seabound numbers into
Slide B's upper-right quadrant callout and cut E. Do not cut B′ to save E.

## Imagery pipeline — instructions for the design agent

Imagery is what makes this sequence feel like a portfolio rather than a
spreadsheet. It is also the fastest way to make it look like a ransom note.
Follow this in order.

### Acquisition, per company

1. **Try the OpenGraph image first.** Fetch the company URL and read
   `og:image`, falling back to `twitter:image`.
2. **Apply the quality bar.** Reject the OG image if it is: smaller than
   1200×630 · a logo on a flat white or light background · generic stock
   photography with no relationship to the product · carrying baked-in
   marketing text, a headline, or a call-to-action · a screenshot of a web page.
3. **If rejected, scrape the site for something illustrative.** Best sources in
   order: the homepage hero image or hero video poster frame; a `/technology`,
   `/product`, `/platform`, `/vessels` or `/about` page; the press or media kit
   page, which often has the highest-resolution assets. What you want is **the
   thing itself** — the vessel, the platform, the robot, the reef — in its
   environment.
4. **If that fails, search public sources.** Prefer, in order: the company's own
   press-kit downloads · Wikimedia Commons · NOAA, ESA, NASA and other public
   agency libraries · Unsplash. The image must be topically honest — an
   autonomous vessel for an autonomous-vessel company, not a generic ocean
   sunset.
5. **If nothing clears the bar, use no image.** A card with a strong typographic
   treatment and no picture is far better than a card with a wrong or ugly one.
   Design the no-image card state deliberately; expect to need it three or four
   times out of eighteen.

### Normalisation — non-negotiable

Eighteen images from eighteen sites will arrive at different aspect ratios,
colour temperatures and brightnesses. Dropped in raw onto `#042632`, they will
look like a collage. Every image gets the same three passes:

1. **Crop** to 16:10, subject-centred.
2. **Duotone toward the brand** — map shadows to `#042632` and highlights to
   `#5FD7C5`, then composite back over the original at **60–70% opacity** so
   some true colour survives. This is the single treatment that makes eighteen
   foreign images read as one system, and it is what lets imagery exist inside a
   deliberately monochromatic palette without breaking it.
3. **Scrim** — a bottom-up linear gradient from `#042632` at 90% opacity to
   transparent across the lower 45% of the image, so the company name is always
   legible regardless of what's underneath.

Judge the result as a set, not one at a time. If one card jumps out of the grid,
fix that card, not the treatment.

### Deliver a manifest

Alongside the slides, produce a table: **company · image URL · acquisition
method (og / scrape / search) · source page · apparent licence**. Someone has to
clear rights before this deck circulates, and reconstructing provenance
afterwards is miserable. Flag anything scraped from a company site as
"permission needed."

### Do not

- **Do not use scraped company logos** anywhere in the sequence. Set company
  names in the deck's own typeface. Eighteen logos at eighteen different weights
  and colours will destroy the page, and logo use implies a relationship that
  doesn't exist.
- **Do not use investor logos** for the same reason, more sharply — see Slide A.
- Do not use AI-generated imagery for real companies' products. If a company's
  vessel is depicted, it should be their vessel.
- Do not put a person's face on a card without a specific reason; these are
  company cards, not team cards.

## Fact-check before this ships

The source material is research output and has not been through a second pass.
These need resolving — several are on-slide claims:

1. **17 vs 18.** The section heading says "17 companies mapped," the table lists
   18 rows, and the summary table says 18. Count is 18 including the five live.
   Fix the heading.
2. **"Companies with US government contracts: 4."** Seasats ($100M+) and Blue
   Water (NAVOCEANO IDIQ) are clear. REGENT has a USMC *demonstration*, not a
   contract; Albacore is *targeting* Navy and SOF customers. Two are solid, two
   are aspirational — restate as "2 holding, 2 in procurement" or drop to 2.
3. **"Level 1 access to the cap table"** — this phrase from the source needs to
   become precise before it goes near a slide. *Access to the team*, *ability to
   participate*, and *committed allocation* are three very different
   representations, and a prospect who takes the strongest reading and later
   finds it was the weakest will not come back. Say exactly which one is true
   for each of the five.
4. **Panthalassa's ~$1B valuation** is "reported." Keep the word "reported" on
   the slide, or drop the number.
5. **Axine's ">99% destruction"** is a company claim. Attribute it or cut it.
6. **Qronon's "100× compute efficiency"** likewise — the source itself notes
   Qronon labels claims by evidence status. Honour that.
7. **Dates.** Several events are stamped 2025–2026; confirm nothing is
   forward-dated relative to the Q3/2026 deck date.
8. **Consistency with slide 10.** That slide anonymises named prospects. This
   one names companies. That's defensible — pipeline funders are being
   protected, and portfolio companies' rounds are largely public — but be ready
   to say so out loud, because an attentive reader will notice the asymmetry.
